# 01 Using this manual and taking responsibility

This manual covers Midwest Drywall Co., Inc. construction, renovation, warehouse, office, and prefab operations. It gives employees work rules and supervisors the controls to put in place before work starts. Site plans, equipment instructions, and task permits supply the details that change from one location to another.

## Stop work when conditions are unsafe

Every employee may stop an unsafe task, move to a safe location, warn others, and contact the foreman or Safety Director. No employee will be retaliated against for a good-faith safety concern, injury report, or stop-work decision. If the foreman is unavailable or the issue remains unresolved, contact the superintendent, Market Leader, or Safety Director. Restart only after the hazard is controlled and the responsible supervisor verifies the correction with affected employees.

## Who does what

| **Role** | **Required action** |
| --- | --- |
| President and regional leadership | Provide resources and hold operating leaders accountable for safe work. |
| Safety Director | Maintain this manual, verify applicable requirements, administer exposure programs, support training, and coordinate serious-incident reporting. |
| Market Leaders and plant leadership | Verify competent-person coverage, equipment readiness, training, inspections, and corrective-action closure. |
| Project managers and superintendents | Resolve GC interfaces, structural loading, permits, access, rescue, and subcontractor coordination before mobilization. |
| Foremen | Conduct daily planning, inspect conditions, verify employee qualifications, and correct or stop unsafe work. |
| Employees | Follow training, inspect assigned equipment, use controls, and report changes, defects, and incidents promptly. |

## Apply the right requirements

Construction activities generally use OSHA 29 CFR 1926; manufacturing and warehouse activities generally use 29 CFR 1910. The activity and exposure determine the rule, not the company name. The Safety Director documents applicability and state-plan requirements before work begins. A GC rule cannot reduce a legal requirement. Follow stricter lawful company or project rules, but never combine incompatible equipment instructions; stop and obtain a safe approved method.

A competent person can recognize hazards and has authority to correct them. A qualified person has demonstrated subject-specific knowledge or credentials. A job title or training card alone does not establish either designation. Assign people by task and document their authority.

## Document control

Program owner: Safety Director. Edition: September 26, 2026. Approval by President or designee: ____________________. Effective date: ____________________. Review at least annually and after regulatory, process, equipment, or serious-incident changes. This approval edition takes effect only after company authorization. Retire superseded copies at rollout. Keep site supplements with the current manual.

# 02 Emergency actions and first aid

Call 911 immediately for a life-threatening injury, fire, collapse, suspected heat stroke, or other emergency. Give the street address, building and floor, access gate, incident type, hazards, and callback number. Send someone to meet responders if safe. Do not delay emergency care while seeking management permission.

## Before work starts

The foreman completes Form F01 with the GC or facility manager. Post it where crews gather and make it accessible without internet service. Each site, office, and plant needs its own routes and site map showing exits, assembly areas, storm shelter, first aid, AEDs if present, extinguishers, and responder access. Provide accessible evacuation arrangements for anyone needing assistance. Review changes with every shift and subcontractor.

Midwest requires at least one currently trained first-aid and CPR employee at each active work location while employees are present. Provide additional coverage for separated work areas, shifts, breaks, and absences. Verify actual response capability and distance to medical services; a name on a roster is not sufficient. No employee is assigned rescue or electrical shutdown duties beyond their training.

## Respond safely

Fire or smoke: sound the alarm, leave by a safe exit, and report to assembly. Only designated trained employees may attempt an incipient-stage fire with a clear escape route. Everyone else evacuates.

Medical emergency: protect the scene and provide only care within your training. Retrieve first aid and an AED if available. Do not move a seriously injured person unless remaining in place presents immediate danger.

Electrical contact: do not touch the person or equipment while energized. Call 911 and have qualified personnel isolate power.

Fall arrest or trapped worker: activate the site rescue plan. Keep others out of the hazard. Do not improvise a rescue that exposes another employee.

Spill or unknown atmosphere: withdraw, isolate the area, and notify the supervisor. Only trained and equipped personnel may handle an incidental spill within their assigned capability.

Threat or violence: move to safety, alert emergency services when needed, and follow the site emergency instructions.

## Account for everyone

The designated coordinator accounts for employees and visitors at assembly. Report missing people and their last known location to responders. No one reenters until the responsible emergency authority releases the area. Preserve evidence after lifesaving actions and hazard control.

Inspect first-aid kits before mobilization and at least weekly on construction sites; restock after use. Provide suitable immediate flushing facilities where corrosive exposure is possible. Maintain AEDs under their manufacturer and local requirements. Practice the EAP at least annually as a Midwest rule and when changes warrant. Record drills and corrective actions.

# 03 Daily planning and coordination

The foreman leads a pre-task discussion before each shift and whenever the task, location, crew, equipment, weather, or nearby work changes. Use Form F02. Employees explain the hazards and controls back in their own words before work starts. A signature without understanding is not enough.

## Plan the work in order

1. Break the task into steps, including deliveries, setup, access, work, cleanup, and demobilization.

2. Identify what could fall, move, collapse, cut, shock, burn, release energy, or create harmful dust, noise, or heat. Consider other trades and people below or behind the work.

3. Eliminate or substitute the hazard first. Then use engineering controls, work rules, and appropriate PPE. PPE is not a substitute for available effective source controls.

4. Identify the responsible person, required training, inspections, permits, rescue arrangements, and equipment for each control.

5. Walk the work area and verify controls before releasing the task. Stop and revise the plan if conditions differ.

## Coordinate before mobilization

Project management obtains the site emergency plan, chemical-hazard information, survey information for existing materials, utility/penetration clearance procedure, access rules, floor-load limits, and concurrent-work schedule. Confirm which employer supplies and maintains shared guardrails, covers, scaffolds, fire protection, and emergency response. Midwest verifies protection for its employees even when the GC supplies it.

For work above others, establish a controlled drop zone and coordinate occupancy below. For occupied buildings and mission-critical facilities, agree on dust containment, negative-air arrangements where specified, debris routes, fire-alarm or suppression impairment control, live electrical boundaries, and owner shutdown permits. Do not disable an alarm or move a barrier without authorization and replacement protection.

## High-risk work needs a written release

Use a task-specific plan for structural framing stability, prefab panel lifting, swing stages, unusual lifts, fall arrest, demolition, penetration into concealed services, and work near energized systems. Identify permits for hot work and approved confined-space evaluation. An ordinary JHA does not replace an engineered erection or bracing design, a written exposure-control plan, or equipment-specific energy-isolation procedure.

## Keep the worksite usable

Maintain clear exits, stairs, electrical working spaces, aisles, and emergency equipment. Supply drinking water, toilets, handwashing, suitable lighting, and clean eating areas. Remove sharp scrap and protruding fasteners promptly. Keep cords and hoses out of travel paths. Protect nearby employees from overhead work, noise, and dust. Review the next day’s hazards before leaving the site.

# 04 Personal protective equipment

The supervisor selects PPE from the task hazard assessment and verifies that each item fits the individual employee and works with the other equipment worn. Construction PPE must properly fit under 1926.95. Plant assessments are documented under 1910.132. Required employer-paid PPE is provided at no cost except lawful payment exceptions; contact Safety or HR before assigning a cost to an employee.

## Select for the actual hazard

| **Exposure** | **Protection and check** |
| --- | --- |
| Falling or flying objects | Appropriate protective headwear; inspect shell and suspension. Follow manufacturer and project chinstrap requirements. |
| Cutting drilling or grinding | Safety glasses with side protection; add goggles or face shield when the task requires it. A face shield alone is insufficient. |
| Metal studs track knives and banding | Task-selected cut-resistant gloves and sleeves where needed. Do not wear gloves where they can entangle in exposed rotating equipment. |
| Falling or rolling material and sharp scrap | Protective footwear appropriate to impact, puncture, and slip hazards. |
| Dust fumes or vapors | Approved engineering controls and the respirator selected under Sections 06 and 07. A dust mask does not protect against gases. |
| Loud tools and equipment | Properly fitted hearing protection selected under Section 08. |
| Vehicle or equipment traffic | High-visibility clothing suited to the traffic plan and applicable roadway requirements. |
| Chemical contact | SDS-based glove material, goggles, protective clothing, and splash protection. “Chemical resistant” alone does not establish suitability. |

## Inspect and demonstrate before use

Employees inspect PPE before each use and report poor fit, contamination, damage, or incompatibility. Remove defective equipment immediately. Do not drill, paint, modify, or attach accessories unless the manufacturer permits it. Store PPE away from heat, sunlight, chemicals, sharp objects, and crushing loads.

Training covers when protection is needed, how to put it on and adjust it, its limitations, inspection, cleaning, replacement, and disposal. The employee demonstrates correct use. Retrain after equipment or job changes and whenever use shows a gap in understanding.

## Specialized protection

Safety selects welding filter shades by process and current, not a single shade for every weld. Fall equipment requires compatible connectors, suitable anchorage, clearance, and a rescue plan. Respirators require a separate determination of medical clearance, fit testing, and training. Electrical PPE does not authorize an unqualified employee to work on energized equipment.

Stop the task if required protection is unavailable, cannot be properly fitted, or interferes with safe use of another required item. Obtain a different size, model, or work method before continuing.

# 05 Hazard communication and chemical safety

The Safety Director administers the written chemical program. Purchasing obtains current safety data sheets before a product is released for use. Each location maintains a product-identifier inventory linked to its SDSs, including joint compounds, adhesives, sealants, firestop products, coatings, cleaners, fuels, and welding consumables. Update the inventory when products change and review it at least annually.

## Read before use

Employees receive understandable training before exposure and whenever a new chemical hazard is introduced. Review labels, pictograms, SDS health effects, handling, storage, ventilation, PPE, emergency actions, and spill limits. Do not rely on odor to judge whether air is safe. Evaluate nonroutine work before starting. Coordinate chemical hazards and SDS access with other employers.

Maintain immediate SDS access during every shift. If access is electronic, provide devices, training, and an outage backup. Employees must not wait for an office to open or a supervisor to supply a password. Keep received labels legible. Do not use food or beverage containers for chemicals.

Secondary containers must identify the product and communicate hazards. Midwest uses supplier-equivalent workplace labels where practicable. The immediate-use exception applies only when the transferring employee controls and uses the material within that shift. A container shared with someone else or left for later needs a label.

## Current transition requirements

The revised Hazard Communication Standard aligns primarily with GHS Revision 7. OSHA extended its transition dates in January 2026. Employers must make necessary workplace-label, program, and additional hazard-training updates for substances by November 20, 2026, and for mixtures by May 19, 2028. Supplier compliance dates are May 19, 2026 for substances and November 19, 2027 for mixtures. New hazards must still be addressed when introduced; these dates do not excuse missing SDSs or inadequate current training.

The Safety Director and Purchasing maintain a transition register of products, SDS revision dates, changed classifications, affected labels, and completed training. Most jobsite compounds are mixtures, but classification must be checked product by product.

## Pictograms are hazard signals

The next page reproduces the OSHA pictogram reference already embedded in the supplied manual, enlarged for reading. The environmental symbol is not mandatory under OSHA. A pictogram does not specify the complete controls; use the product label and SDS. Keep spill response within employee training and equipment capability. Unknown, spreading, or hazardous releases require isolation and emergency assistance.

# 06 Silica and airborne dust controls

The Safety Director maintains the silica exposure-control plan; the foreman implements it and a designated silica competent person performs frequent and regular inspections. Include drywall sanding, routing, mixing powders, cleanup, anchor drilling, concrete cutting or grinding, fiber-cement work, and exposures created by other trades. Do not assume gypsum products are silica-free; check the SDS and exposure evidence.

## Select a valid control method before work

For construction, use 1926.1153 Table 1 only when the exact tool, task, location, duration, dust controls, and required respiratory protection match the listed conditions. Otherwise use a documented exposure assessment and effective controls. The action level is 25 micrograms per cubic meter and the PEL is 50, each as an eight-hour time-weighted average. The PEL is a legal limit, not a guarantee of no health risk.

| **Task** | **Required planning decision** |
| --- | --- |
| Concrete anchor drilling | Verify the exact Table 1 drill/shroud/dust-collector arrangement or obtain an alternative exposure assessment. Include safe hole cleaning. |
| Fiber-cement cutting | Verify the outdoor small-blade Table 1 conditions or assess the actual method. Indoor cutting is not covered by that outdoor entry. |
| Drywall sanding routing and mixing | Use current representative objective data or monitoring. Use effective extraction, low-dust methods, and controlled mixing; do not invent a Table 1 category. |
| Cleanup | Use HEPA vacuuming or wet methods. Restrict access and control dust during disposal and filter changes. |

## Historical data need validation

The supplied manual describes sampling from 2017 and 2018, including one above-PEL sanding result. Retain the original reports. They may support a current assessment only if the materials, tools, controls, work methods, duration, and environment remain representative. A five-foot sanding pole or sweeping compound alone is not proof that today’s exposures are below the action level. Do not carry forward the blanket statement that no task requires a respirator.

## Control the work area

Capture dust at the source; maintain shrouds, filters, airflow, and water delivery. Isolate dust-producing work from nearby crews and occupied areas. No dry sweeping or dry brushing that could contribute to silica exposure unless wet methods, HEPA vacuuming, or other exposure-minimizing methods are not feasible. No compressed-air cleaning unless the standard’s limited conditions are met and Safety approves. A sweeping compound does not automatically satisfy that exception.

Stop when extraction fails, dust escapes uncontrolled, required respiratory protection is missing, or the task changes. Use Form F03 to document the task-specific plan and access restrictions. Review the plan at least annually and after changes.

# 06A Silica assessment and medical administration

Safety must maintain the exposure evidence and surveillance arrangements that support the field controls. A checklist or the employee manual alone does not establish exposure compliance.

## Construction exposure assessment

Under the scheduled-monitoring option, initial sampling represents the highest likely exposed employee for each relevant shift, job classification, and work area. Repeat at least every six months when results are at or above the action level but at or below the PEL; at least every three months when above the PEL. Apply the standard’s below-action-level discontinuation rules, including two consecutive results at least seven days apart when required. Reassess when changes may create new or increased exposure. The performance option requires adequate objective data or monitoring that accurately characterizes exposure.

Notify affected construction employees of assessment results within five working days of completing the assessment, and describe corrective action when above the PEL. Employees or designated representatives may observe monitoring with required protection. An industrial hygienist should determine the appropriate sampling method and interpretation.

## Medical surveillance

Construction surveillance is required when silica rules require respirator use on 30 or more days per year. General industry surveillance uses exposure at or above the action level on 30 or more days per year. Do not apply the construction trigger to a prefab plant. Record qualifying days across assignments.

Arrange required initial examinations within 30 days of assignment, subject to the standard’s recent-examination exception, and periodic examinations at least every three years or sooner if the clinician recommends. The clinician conducts the required history, examination, chest X-ray, spirometry, initial TB test, and other indicated tests. Supply the clinician with the task, exposure, PPE, and previous-exam information required by the applicable standard.

Medical clearance for respirator use is a separate requirement and must precede required respirator use. Do not wait 30 days to obtain respirator clearance.

## Privacy and follow-up

The clinician gives the employee the medical report. Midwest receives only the permitted written medical opinion and authorized information, not unrestricted clinical records. Implement work and respirator limitations and specialist referrals promptly. Keep medical records confidential and separate from routine incident and personnel files.

## Plant applicability and records

For manufacturing or shop processes, apply 1910.1053, including exposure assessment, regulated areas where required, and its notification and surveillance rules. Construction Table 1 is not a blanket exemption for plant operations. Retain exposure records for at least 30 years and medical records for employment plus 30 years, subject to applicable exceptions. Keep objective data for the period relied upon. Review findings with affected workers and the plant manager.

# 07 Respiratory protection

The Safety Director is the respiratory program administrator and selects NIOSH-approved respirators from a documented exposure assessment. Selection addresses the contaminant, concentration, oxygen level, assigned protection factor, physical form, cartridge or filter, and work conditions. Old brand or model lists do not establish suitability. No Midwest employee may enter an unknown, oxygen-deficient, or immediately dangerous atmosphere under this manual.

## Required respirator use

Before use, provide confidential medical evaluation, any necessary follow-up, training, and fit testing for tight-fitting respirators. Fit test before first use, at least annually, when the make/model/style/size changes, and when physical changes can affect fit. The employee must be clean-shaven where the face seal contacts the skin and perform a user seal check each time the respirator is donned. A seal check does not replace a fit test.

The administrator specifies filters, change schedules, cleaning, storage, inspection, and replacement. For gas/vapor cartridges, use a suitable end-of-service-life indicator or an objective change schedule; odor, taste, or irritation is not the change-out plan. Leave the exposure area immediately if breathing becomes difficult, a seal fails, breakthrough is suspected, or equipment is damaged. Replace or correct the equipment in clean air.

Train at least annually and sooner when changes or poor use require it. Address limitations, emergencies, inspection, medical symptoms, cleaning, and maintenance. Inspect routine-use equipment before each use and during cleaning. Clean and disinfect shared equipment before another person uses it. Store clean respirators protected from distortion and contamination. Evaluate the program with users and correct deficiencies.

## Voluntary use is a separate decision

Safety first determines that use is truly voluntary and does not create a hazard. Provide the complete OSHA Appendix D information in Form F10. Voluntary use of filtering facepieces alone does not require the full written program, medical evaluation, or fit testing under 1910.134. Voluntary use of elastomeric respirators requires the necessary medical and maintenance elements of a written program. Required filtering-facepiece use does require the full applicable program.

Do not label respirator use “voluntary” when it is required by an exposure assessment, Table 1, a project requirement, or Midwest. Loose-fitting respirators still require appropriate medical evaluation and training when required for protection, although tight-fitting facepiece fit-testing requirements do not apply to them.

## Records

Keep fit-test records until the next test, medical-clearance documentation with restricted access, and the selection basis, training, and program evaluations. Maintain a current approved-equipment register rather than obsolete model numbers. Required respirators, evaluations, testing, and training are provided at company expense.

# 08 Noise and hearing conservation

Evaluate metal cutting, impact tools, powder-actuated tools, concrete drilling, compressors, production lines, and noise from other trades. Use quieter equipment, isolation, maintenance, and reduced exposure time before relying solely on hearing protection. Measure representative exposure when hazardous noise is reasonably possible; a phone app or being able to talk is not a substitute for an adequate assessment.

## Construction and plant requirements differ

| **Setting** | **Regulatory baseline and Midwest action** |
| --- | --- |
| Construction | 1926.52 uses a 90 dBA eight-hour permissible exposure with shorter allowable durations as noise increases. Implement a continuing effective hearing-conservation program when exposures exceed its table. Midwest also uses 85 dBA eight-hour TWA as a preventive trigger for assessment, training, and hearing protection. |
| Manufacturing and general industry | At an 85 dBA eight-hour TWA, 1910.95 requires the hearing-conservation program, including monitoring, audiometry, protection availability, training, and records. Feasible controls apply when its permissible limits are exceeded. |

## Employee rules

Wear the selected hearing protection in designated areas and for posted tasks. Fit plugs or muffs correctly and keep them clean. Do not substitute music earbuds for hearing protection. Tell the supervisor if protection prevents hearing alarms or communication; arrange a safe communication method. Report ringing, muffled hearing, or poor fit promptly.

## Program administration

Safety arranges dosimetry that represents the actual workday, explains results, and checks that protectors reduce exposure adequately. Account for simultaneous equipment, long shifts, impulse noise, and changes in production. Do not simply subtract the package NRR from the measured level. Mark high-noise areas and supply suitable choices of protection at no cost.

For the general-industry program, obtain a valid baseline audiogram within six months of first qualifying exposure, subject to the mobile-test-van exception, and an annual audiogram thereafter. Apply required pre-test quiet periods and compare results with baseline. Notify an employee of a standard threshold shift in writing within 21 days of determination and arrange required refitting, retraining, evaluation, and follow-up. Evaluate OSHA recordability separately.

Provide annual hearing-conservation training to covered employees. Retain noise exposure measurements for two years and audiometric records for employment duration under 1910.95; apply longer overlapping requirements where applicable. Have an occupational health professional establish any construction audiometry program from the exposure findings and Midwest’s preventive policy.

## Before release at each plant

Complete and document a noise survey of actual production. Determine covered job classifications, testing provider, protector selection, and monitoring schedule. A plant is not declared below the action level without supporting evidence.

# 09 Falls openings and rescue

Identify exposure at perimeter edges, shafts, stairs, roofs, loading points, floor holes, and equipment access before releasing the area. The foreman verifies the protective system and the competent or qualified person required for its design, installation, and inspection. Do not treat unfinished studs or an unverified cable as a guardrail.

## Choose the correct fall threshold

| **Work situation** | **Protection trigger** |
| --- | --- |
| Ordinary construction edges | Protect at 6 feet or more above a lower level under Subpart M. Other hazards may require protection at lower heights. |
| General-industry floors and platforms | Protect at 4 feet or more under 1910.28, with applicable task-specific exceptions and additional requirements. |
| Supported scaffolds | Midwest requires protection above 6 feet, earlier than OSHA’s general scaffold threshold above 10 feet. Use lower manufacturer limits when specified. |
| Swing stages and MEWPs | Use the equipment-specific protections in Sections 12 and 13; do not apply an ordinary floor-height rule. |
| Holes and dangerous equipment | Guard or cover holes against stepping, tripping, falling, and falling objects as applicable. Protect against dangerous equipment even where an ordinary height trigger is not reached. |

## Install a complete system

For ordinary construction guardrails, top rails are 42 inches plus or minus 3 inches, with intermediate protection. Design for at least 200 pounds at the top rail and 150 pounds at intermediate members under the standard’s loading conditions. Use properly designed posts, connections, and substrate attachments. Nominal lumber or metal-stud size alone is not a design.

Secure and mark hole covers “HOLE” or “COVER,” or color-code as permitted. Covers must support at least twice the anticipated employee, equipment, and material load; roadway covers must support twice the largest anticipated vehicle axle load. Protect employees below with toeboards, screening, secure tools, and exclusion zones as needed. Pedestrian rails and toeboards are not vehicle barriers.

## Personal fall arrest

Use a full-body harness and compatible system. Anchorage must meet the 5,000-pound-per-person criterion or be designed, installed, and used under a qualified person as part of a complete system with the required safety factor. Never use light-gauge framing, sprinkler pipe, ceiling grid, or guardrails as an assumed anchor. Evaluate free fall, arrest distance, worker height, lifeline stretch, swing fall, sharp edges, and manufacturer clearance. Inspect before use; quarantine equipment subjected to a fall.

Plan prompt rescue before exposure using Form F05. Identify trained rescuers, equipment, communication, access, and a practical retrieval method. Calling 911 alone is not an adequate plan unless response capability and timing have been established. Rehearse the method without exposing anyone to a fall.

# 09A Roof edges and structural work boundaries

Midwest retains a 15-foot roof-edge setback for planning, travel, and material storage unless approved protective systems are provided. A setback or warning line is not a general substitute for required fall protection. Provide conventional protection for ordinary drywall, framing, sheathing, and façade work exposed to unprotected edges. Keep materials secured against wind and rolling or sliding movement.

## Do not borrow another trade’s exception

Controlled decking zones and steel-erection connector rules are not permissions for drywall crews to work unprotected. Before any work potentially covered by OSHA Subpart R, the Safety Director and qualified erection leadership determine applicability and issue a separate task-specific erection and fall-protection plan. Do not assume cold-formed framing is either always included or always excluded.

A fall-protection plan under 1926.502(k) is available only for qualifying leading-edge, precast-concrete, or residential work with the required infeasibility or greater-hazard demonstration. It must be prepared by a qualified person and implemented under a competent person. An ordinary foreman-written plan does not waive conventional protection for other work. This manual does not authorize an alternative fall-protection method by default.

## Openings and temporary removal

Before removing a rail or cover for a delivery or installation, establish the replacement protection, exclusion zone, responsible attendant where needed, and restoration plan. Protect the employee handling the load. Restore the permanent system as soon as the opening is no longer needed. Do not leave a gap unattended or allow another crew to discover it without warning.

## Verify rescue and anchorage

Site-specific drawings must identify anchor locations and limitations. A roof’s capacity for stored materials does not prove anchorage capacity. Remove equipment with exposed conductors, missing ground pins, damaged insulation, overheated connections, or defective protection. No tape-only field repair of an unsafe cord.

Use grounded or double-insulated tools as designed. Protect cables from doors, water, sharp metal, and vehicle traffic. Do not use temporary cords as permanent wiring. Keep required electrical working spaces clear of drywall bundles, carts, studs, and tools. Do not open covers or move temporary wiring without authorization.

## Hidden services

Before cutting, drilling, coring, fastening, or demolition, obtain the project’s clearance for utilities, post-tension cables, and embedded services. Confirm the actual location and penetration depth using the required investigation and scanning method. Stop at conflicting information or unexpected material. Do not substitute an employee’s guess for the release process.

Use the applicable utility-locate process before excavation or ground disturbance, including 811 where required. Public utility markings do not necessarily locate private or interior services. Have the responsible party locate those too.

## Overhead utilities

Maintain the required clearance for the particular equipment and voltage. For ordinary work, the company baseline is at least 10 feet from lines rated 50 kV or less, with greater distances where applicable. Crane rules require the separate process in Section 19. Never assume a line is insulated or deenergized. Stop when voltage or clearance cannot be established. Consider long studs, scaffolds, ladders, panels, and load swing as well as the machine itself.

## Isolation and emergency response

Use Section 23 for hazardous energy. Qualified electrical personnel verify absence of voltage using appropriate equipment and procedures. A switch in the off position is not proof of isolation. Do not touch an electrocuted person still in contact with energy. Call 911, keep people away, and have power isolated by qualified personnel before trained responders approach.

# 23 Lockout and stored energy

Use an equipment-specific energy-control procedure before servicing or maintenance where unexpected startup or release can injure someone. The plant program follows 1910.147 where applicable; construction equipment and electrical work follow applicable construction rules with equivalent safe isolation planning. Only trained and authorized employees apply and verify isolation.

## Isolate before reaching into the hazard

1. Review the procedure and identify every source: electrical, pneumatic, hydraulic, gravity, springs, thermal energy, batteries, and stored pressure. Notify affected employees.

2. Shut down normally. Operate each energy-isolating device; an emergency stop, software command, or interlock is not an isolating device.

3. Apply personal locks and identification at every required point. Use lockable isolation where available; tagout-only arrangements need the applicable equivalent protection and authorization.

4. Drain, block, bleed, discharge, restrain, or otherwise control stored energy. Support raised tables, booms, and panels mechanically. Monitor energy that can reaccumulate.

5. Verify isolation before work. Try normal controls safely and return them to off or neutral. Qualified personnel conduct electrical testing where required.

6. Perform the task without defeating protection. For testing that requires temporary energization, follow the authorized removal, clear-out, test, shutdown, and reapplication sequence.

7. Before restoration, inspect the area, reinstall guards, confirm employees are clear, remove locks under the procedure, notify affected employees, and restore energy safely.

## Personal protection across crews and shifts

Each authorized employee controls their own lock. Group isolation uses a documented method that gives each exposed employee equivalent personal protection, such as a lockbox. Coordinate with outside contractors and verify their isolation procedure. Incoming employees establish protection before outgoing employees remove theirs during a shift transfer.

Only the employee who applied a lock normally removes it. An exceptional employer removal requires a written procedure that verifies absence, makes reasonable efforts to contact the employee, and ensures the employee knows before returning to work. Supervisor convenience is not an exception.

## Training inspection and records

Train authorized employees in source recognition and isolation methods; train affected and other employees not to restart or tamper with locked equipment. Retrain when assignments, equipment, procedures, or performance change. Under the plant program, inspect each energy-control procedure at least annually through an authorized employee other than those using that procedure, correct deficiencies, and certify the inspection.

Keep current machine-specific procedures, training certifications, annual inspection records, and group/shift-transfer arrangements. The limited cord-and-plug exception applies only when unplugging controls the hazard and the plug remains under the servicing employee’s exclusive control. Do not assume every portable tool qualifies.

# 24 Fire prevention and hot work

Keep combustibles, flammables, ignition sources, and evacuation routes under control. Provide accessible extinguishers selected for the hazards and required distribution. On construction floors, apply the 2A per 3,000-square-foot and 100-foot travel-distance requirements, floor/stair provisions, and additional flammable-liquid/gas protection as applicable. Inspect and service extinguishers under the governing requirements; Midwest uses documented monthly visual checks and required annual maintenance.

## Hot work needs authorization

Welding, burning, brazing, spark-producing grinding, and other ignition-producing work outside a designated safe hot-work area require Form F04 or the GC’s equivalent approved permit. Identify the exact location, task, authorized period, operator, authorizer, fire watches, and post-work checks. Renew when the shift, location, or hazards change.

Remove or effectively protect combustibles; inspect at least 35 feet around the work and anywhere sparks, heat, or slag may travel. Check the opposite side of walls and floors, roof spaces, shafts, penetrations, and hidden cavities. Protect openings and nearby people. Have suitable extinguishing equipment immediately available. Do not issue a permit where flammable atmospheres, uncontrolled dust, unavailable fire protection, or inaccessible combustible cavities prevent safe work.

## Fire-watch duties

Use a trained dedicated fire watch when combustible exposure, openings, heat conduction, falling sparks, or the permit require it. Assign additional watches for areas one person cannot see. The watch has no distracting duties, knows the alarm method, can stop work, and attempts extinguishment only within training and a safe escape route.

Midwest retains a minimum 30-minute watch after work finishes, extended for the actual hazard and any longer GC, insurer, adopted fire-code, or permit requirement. This is not a universal statement that 30 minutes satisfies every construction hot-work condition. The authorizer records the selected duration and any later monitoring. Inspect adjoining and concealed exposures and document closure. Recheck after breaks and never leave a smoldering hazard unattended.

## Storage and heaters

Use approved containers and assigned ventilation-controlled flammable storage. Secure gas cylinders upright, protect valves, keep oxygen free of oil and grease, and separate stored oxygen from fuel gas/combustibles by 20 feet or a compliant 5-foot, half-hour-rated barrier. Move cylinders using approved carriers, never by caps or improvised slings.

Use heaters listed for the setting; observe clearances, ventilation, stability, and flame-failure controls. Secure combustible coverings against movement. No solid-fuel salamanders indoors or on scaffolds. LP-gas storage inside occupied buildings requires specific lawful approval. Evaluate carbon monoxide; an open door alone is not proof of adequate ventilation.

# 25 Welding cutting and fumes

Only trained and authorized employees weld or cut. Review the hot-work permit, material coatings, SDSs, electrical supply, ventilation, adjacent workers, and fall exposure before starting. Keep welding away from chlorinated-solvent vapors and combustible atmospheres. Never use oxygen to ventilate a space or clean clothing. Do not weld or cut a drum, tank, or closed container without a separately engineered cleaning, testing, venting, and work authorization process. Ordinary field employees must stop and refer that work to Safety.

Resume only after the supervisor and Safety resolve the condition. # 26 Heat cold and severe weather

The foreman evaluates temperature, humidity, radiant heat, workload, clothing, acclimatization, and conditions inside unfinished buildings and plants. Provide readily accessible drinking water, cool rest areas, work/rest arrangements, and active employee observation. Federal OSHA’s heat-rule page continues to describe a proposed rule as of this review; this chapter’s preventive practices are Midwest rules, not a claim that proposed federal numeric triggers are already final. Apply state and local requirements where relevant.

## Heat prevention and response

Encourage frequent small drinks and ready access to rest. Increase rest and reduce work intensity as heat stress rises. Schedule strenuous work during cooler periods and use ventilation or cooling where effective. Watch new, returning, and reassigned workers closely. For new workers, OSHA recommends beginning with no more than 20 percent of usual heat exposure on day one and increasing by no more than 20 percent daily; tailor acclimatization with Safety and occupational-health advice. Experienced returning workers also need an appropriate ramp-up after an absence.

Use a buddy system. Stop for headache, dizziness, nausea, unusual weakness, cramps, or heavy fatigue. Move the person to a cool place and obtain trained assistance. Confusion, collapse, seizure, or suspected heat stroke is an emergency: call 911 and begin rapid cooling within training while help is coming. Do not leave the person alone or give fluids to someone not fully alert. Sweating does not rule out heat stroke.

## Cold conditions

Provide warm breaks, dry layered clothing, traction, and shelter from wind. Replace wet clothing and monitor for numbness, loss of coordination, slurred speech, or confusion. Move affected employees to warmth and obtain medical help. Do not rub frostbitten skin or expose it to intense direct heat. Use heaters only under Section 24.

## Weather stop rules

Midwest retains these company limits: stop elevated outdoor work at 30 mph winds or gusts; stop work and take shelter at 35 mph. Stop earlier whenever equipment ratings, panel control, or the task require it. These ceilings are not safe operating ratings.

At thunder or observed lightning, stop outdoor exposed work and move to a substantial building or suitable enclosed vehicle. Wait at least 30 minutes after the last thunder or lightning before release. During a tornado watch, stop elevated work, review shelter arrangements, and suspend work where adequate shelter is unavailable. During a warning, go immediately to shelter. Do not delay shelter to secure materials.

For flooding, leave the hazard and never enter moving water or an energized wet area. After severe weather, inspect access, structures, scaffolds, electrical systems, and equipment before the responsible supervisor authorizes restart.

# 27 Bloodborne pathogens and exposure response

The Safety Director identifies job classifications and tasks with reasonably anticipated occupational exposure, including designated first-aid responders and assigned cleanup personnel. Maintain the written exposure determination and facility-specific exposure-control plan without assuming PPE eliminates exposure classification. Review at least annually and when tasks or technology change, including required employee input on effective safer devices where applicable.

## Protect before helping

Provide appropriate gloves, eye/face protection, barriers, handwashing, approved disinfectants, and regulated-waste containers at no cost. Use tools, not bare hands, to handle potentially contaminated sharp debris. Do not bend, recap, or handle contaminated needles except under a specifically lawful procedure. No untrained employee cleans up an exposure scene.

Wash hands after glove removal and exposure. Restrict contaminated areas until trained personnel clean them using the product’s required contact time and safe method. Handle laundry and waste to prevent leaks, punctures, and unnecessary contact. Never eat or drink in exposure work areas.

## Vaccination and training

For covered employees, make hepatitis B vaccination available at no cost after required training and within 10 working days of initial assignment, unless the standard’s applicable exceptions are met. A post-exposure declaration does not replace that form.

Provide initial and at least annual interactive training by a knowledgeable person, with an opportunity for questions. Include the standard, exposure plan, routes of transmission, controls, PPE, vaccination, emergencies, post-exposure steps, and signs/labels. Maintain training records for three years.

## Exposure response

Immediately wash affected skin with soap and water and flush mucous membranes with water. Report promptly and obtain confidential medical evaluation and follow-up as soon as possible. Do not delay for completion of paperwork. Document the route, circumstances, task, and available source information while protecting privacy.

The employer arranges the required confidential evaluation, legally permitted source testing, employee testing, counseling, indicated prophylaxis, and follow-up at no cost. The clinician handles consent and clinical decisions. Supply the clinician with required exposure and vaccination information. Provide the employee the healthcare professional’s limited written opinion within the required 15-day period after evaluation; other findings remain confidential.

Maintain covered medical records for employment plus 30 years under the applicable rules, separately from general records. The employee’s report or signature never waives medical care, reporting rights, or benefits.

# 28 Confined spaces and existing hazardous materials

Midwest does not authorize permit-required confined-space entry through this manual. Employees must not enter or attempt a rescue in a permit space. Obtain host/GC space information before work. A competent person identifies and evaluates construction confined spaces and permit-space hazards; the Safety Director coordinates the applicable construction or general-industry requirements.

## Recognize and control a space

A confined space is large enough to enter and work, has limited or restricted entry/exit, and is not designed for continuous occupancy. Pits, crawl spaces, tanks, vaults, and some service spaces can qualify. Hazardous atmosphere, engulfment, entrapment geometry, or another serious hazard can make it permit-required. A space does not become safe simply because a person can fit through the opening.

Label or otherwise communicate permit-space hazards and prevent unauthorized entry. Entry includes breaking the plane of the opening with any part of the body. Do not lean into a space to test it. Only a documented evaluation can establish a non-permit space or a lawful reclassification; ventilation alone does not eliminate every hazard.

Non-permit entry requires written Safety authorization, the evaluation basis, entrant names, communication, access, and check-in/out arrangements on Form F08. Stop and exit if conditions change. If permit entry is needed, engage a qualified specialty provider or develop and approve a complete separate entry and rescue program before assignment.

## Renovation demolition and suspect materials

Before disturbing existing walls, ceilings, coatings, flooring, insulation, or fireproofing, obtain the owner/GC survey and task-specific assessment for asbestos, lead, and other relevant hazards. Do not presume a newer-looking material is safe. Stop and isolate suspect material until a qualified determination is provided. Ordinary dust-control training does not authorize asbestos disturbance or abatement.

Lead-painted substrates can create exposure during cutting, welding, drilling, and demolition. Safety evaluates 1926.62, including assessment and interim protections where required. Do not apply the silica plan as a substitute for lead or asbestos controls. Mold, contaminated dust, bird/bat waste, and other biological hazards also require task-specific review.

## Structure and utilities

Before demolition, obtain the required competent-person engineering survey and written evidence under 1926.850, isolate utilities, and establish sequence, support, fall protection, and exclusion zones. Do not remove a wall or brace based only on appearance. Locate concealed electrical, gas, water, and post-tension systems before penetration. Leave regulated abatement, unknown atmospheres, and unauthorized structural changes to properly qualified and approved personnel.

# 29 Driving and fleet authorization

# 30 Commercial vehicles and DOT testing

The fleet administrator documents which vehicles and operations are regulated. An interstate property-carrying CMV may be subject to safety rules at 10,001 pounds even when no CDL is required. CDL weight thresholds generally begin at 26,001 pounds, with combination/towed-unit distinctions and passenger or placarded-hazardous-material triggers. A dispatcher cannot order an unlawful trip.

## Post-crash decisions and clocks

A fatal crash triggers testing of a surviving covered driver. A crash involving immediate medical treatment away from the scene or disabling damage requiring tow-away triggers testing when the driver receives the qualifying moving-violation citation within the applicable window: 8 hours for alcohol and 32 hours for drugs. DOT disqualification and employment discipline are separate decisions. Apply Parts 40 and 382, including record-specific retention and confidentiality.

Maintain accessible public routes and emergency exits. Keep stored material from reducing sight lines or turning space. Establish speeds suitable for congestion, visibility, load, and surface conditions.

Only trained and authorized flaggers direct public traffic. Provide the appropriate high-visibility apparel, STOP/SLOW paddle or authorized signaling method, visible position, advance warning, communication, and escape route. Do not combine flagging with a distracting duty. Place spotters outside the vehicle path and crush zone and stop movement if communication is lost.

## Protect adjacent occupants and trades

Control overhead installation and falling-object exposure with secure materials, exclusion zones, screening, or engineered protection. Coordinate access below before removing cladding or ceiling material. Preserve fire egress and accessible routes during each phase. For occupied healthcare or data-center work, use the owner-approved dust, contamination, utility, and impairment procedures. Stop on a failed barrier, dust escape, or unplanned exposure.

## Waste and releases

Estimate waste streams before mobilization and provide suitable containers for sharp metal, board, packaging, chemicals, batteries, lamps, and regulated materials. Do not mix hazardous waste with ordinary debris. Use approved recycling and disposal routes and retain required disposal records. Keep waste out of drains and soil. Provide spill supplies appropriate to employees’ authorized incidental-cleanup role.

Unknown substances, uncontrolled leaks, or releases beyond training require withdrawal and escalation. Only qualified personnel classify and manage hazardous waste or conduct emergency spill response. Do not wash a chemical spill into a drain or remove hazardous-material labels before disposal is approved.

## Shutdown and restart

Secure materials and equipment at shift end, inspect temporary barriers, and leave routes safe for other occupants. After an incident, delivery change, public complaint, or altered traffic pattern, reassess controls before restarting. Report damage or exposure involving the public immediately to Safety and project management and preserve factual evidence.

# F10 Voluntary respirator information and receipt

Safety must first determine that use is voluntary and does not create a hazard. This receipt does not waive employer obligations or authorize respirator use in an unassessed exposure.

## OSHA Appendix D information

Respirators are an effective method of protection against designated hazards when properly selected and worn. Respirator use is encouraged, even when exposures are below the exposure limit, to provide an additional level of comfort and protection for workers. However, if a respirator is used improperly or not kept clean, the respirator itself can become a hazard to the worker. Sometimes, workers may wear respirators to avoid exposures to hazards, even if the amount of hazardous substance does not exceed the limits set by OSHA standards. If your employer provides respirators for your voluntary use, or if you provide your own respirator, you need to take certain precautions to be sure that the respirator itself does not present a hazard.

You should do the following:

1. Read and heed all instructions provided by the manufacturer on use, maintenance, cleaning and care, and warnings regarding the respirators limitations.

2. Choose respirators certified for use to protect against the contaminant of concern. NIOSH, the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health of the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, certifies respirators. A label or statement of certification should appear on the respirator or respirator packaging. It will tell you what the respirator is designed for and how much it will protect you.

3. Do not wear your respirator into atmospheres containing contaminants for which your respirator is not designed to protect against. For example, a respirator designed to filter dust particles will not protect you against gases, vapors, or very small solid particles of fumes or smoke.

4. Keep track of your respirator so that you do not mistakenly use someone else's respirator.

Source: 29 CFR 1910.134 Appendix D. Federal mandatory information reproduced above.
